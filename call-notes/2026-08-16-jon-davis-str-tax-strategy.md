## The tax mechanics covered

### Excess Business Loss (EBL) limit — IRC §461(l)
- **Single filer: $256,000 max** business loss usable against W-2 income.
- **Married filing jointly: $512,000.**
- This is the ceiling regardless of how much depreciation the deal throws off.
- Applies to *combined* business losses — so if Sam's side hustle is already showing a $50–100k loss, that eats into the same $256k bucket. **His CPA needs to account for that.**
- If income is coming through a business that's *making* money, the loss can be infinite. The cap only bites on pure W-2 income.

### The NOL rollover — the part that unlocks more
This was Jon's opening point and it's the strategic piece worth remembering:

> Anything above the EBL limit doesn't evaporate — it **rolls forward as a Net Operating Loss (NOL)**, and next year you can use it to offset up to **80% of income**, *stacked on top of* a fresh EBL limit.

**Jon's worked example (single filer, $1M W-2, $1M of depreciation):**
- Year 1: take $256k. The other ~$750k rolls forward as NOL.
- Year 2: 80% of income can be reduced by that NOL.

**Jon's other example (his client — wife makes $1.5M/yr, wants to offset more than $512k):**
- Can't do it in a single year.
- But: generate $1M in depreciation this year → take $512k now, ~$488k rolls to NOL.
- Next year, invest only $400–500k, get a *fresh* $512k EBL limit, and take the ~$480k NOL **on top** of it.
- Net effect: you can combine the two in the following year. **Plan ahead and the ceiling roughly doubles.**

Jon repeated this later for Sam with a cleaner number: a $350k loss against a $250k-ish limit leaves $100k carrying over; do another investment next year and you're at the limit again *plus* the $100k sitting there.

### Material participation / the STR exception
- Default IRS treatment of real estate = **passive**. Passive losses only offset passive income — useless against W-2.
- **Exception:** if average rental period is **7 days or less** AND you materially participate → non-passive status → losses offset W-2, 1099, business income, RSUs, commissions, whatever.
- Aaron's framing on the hours: **100+ hours in year one.** Ways to get there: building the guidebook, visiting the property, helping set it up, staying there over weekends. That's what most clients do.
- Jon: aside from those hours, you do *none* of the work. No painting, no furniture assembly, no guest contact, no pricing.
- Aaron cited **§469** as the governing code section for the passive rules.

### Cost seg / depreciation
- They run a **cost segregation study** on each property — the report that quantifies available depreciation.
- Ratio is consistently **~1.3–1.4x of capital invested** returned as write-off. $100k in → $130–140k loss. Holds up and down the size range.
- Investor receives a **K-1** showing the loss; CPA plugs it in as a **non-passive** loss. Jon flagged this is where CPAs tend to ask questions.

### Look-back depreciation (side note for Zach)
Aaron flagged to Zach: you can **retroactively capture depreciation on properties you already own** — go back years. He has a client who recovered **$30k** on a property bought four years ago that he didn't know he could touch. Zach said he's already discussed this with his CPA and plans to use it.

## Jon's business model / deal structure

**What they do:** find, underwrite, negotiate, and get under contract properties that pencil as short-term rentals; financing already secured; investor comes in as the capital partner.

| Element | Terms |
|---|---|
| Investors per deal | **Exactly one** — this is the whole ballgame (see below) |
| Investor time commitment | ~100 hours, year one only |
| Management fee | **None** |
| Capital calls / additional funds | **None ever.** Capex risk sits with Jon's side. |
| Cash flow to investor | **6%, fixed**, paid monthly regardless of season, regulation, or shutdown |
| Investor equity | **50%–75%** depending on deal (example was 70%) |
| Operator's comp | Their retained equity at exit (~30%) — no fees along the way |
| Hold period | **8-year model**, 7–10 year exit window |
| Appreciation assumption | **3.5%/yr** baked into projections (conservative — upside flows to investor) |
| Down payment | ~10% down; they raise **20% of purchase price** |
| Portfolio size | **300+ properties** nationwide |
| Track record | **Never missed a distribution in 10 years** |
| Perk | **7 free nights/yr** at your property; tradeable at equivalent ADR into any other property in the portfolio |

**Where the other 10% goes:** closing costs, part of acquisition cost, minor refurbishment, extra amenities to lift revenue (they upgrade even existing Airbnbs), and a small holding account for future repairs / cash-flow shortfalls. All of it is baked into the projections.

**Exit math:** fair market value − mortgage = equity, split by the ownership percentage.

### Why one investor per deal matters
This is the answer to Zach's syndication question and the single most important structural fact:

> With more than one person in the deal, **nobody** can take the tax benefits. Multiple investors → you can't meet the material participation requirement → it's passive → the depreciation only offsets passive income and does nothing for W-2. At that point it's just any other real estate syndication.

Two things must both be true for the strategy to work: **(1) short-term rental, (2) one investor who materially participates.**

### How the 6% is actually backstopped (Zach's question)
- Each property is **underwritten to self-sustain** — it should cash flow enough on its own to cover the distribution, repairs, and still make Jon money. It's not pooled.
- Each property carries a **capital account** built up to cover monthly distributions.
- If a property *and* its capital account fall short, **Jon's company pays out of pocket** from the broader portfolio.
- The 300-property portfolio smooths seasonality (e.g. Florida beach houses are dead in Jan/Feb) — they view returns annually and portfolio-wide.
- Jon's characterization: the portfolio is an **extra layer of protection**, not the source of the distribution. Sam's money isn't invested in the portfolio.
- On underwriting discipline: "We had a few properties in the early years that were like we're just super excited to buy a house, so we bought the house. But now it's all data driven, and if it does not fit our buy box, we just don't make an exception."

### Deal visibility (Zach's question)
Investors get shown deals and choose — "I'll put deals in front of you and say, hey, do you want to take this deal or not." Debt terms disclosed on request.
